Who gets a call?

A college wants to help students before they fail. A computer program (an AI model) looks at each student's attendance and marks and gives a risk score from 0 to 99. A high score means “this student may fail”.

The support team cannot call everyone. So you draw a line, the cut-off. Every student whose score is at or above the cut-off gets a call from the support team.

Every student falls into one of four boxes. “Needed help” means the student was really at risk: without help, they would fail.
Called, and really needed help. Good. Called, but was fine. An unnecessary call. Not called, but needed help. Missed: no help came. Not called, and was fine. Good.

Everything on this page is made up for teaching. No real student data is used.

Game rules: “the same” means the two groups are within 4 points of each other, and each group must have between 10% and 40% called, because that is all the support team can handle. These are rules for this classroom game, not official fairness standards. “Done” means you hit the target. It does not mean the system is now fair.

Why can't challenge 3 be done? (open after you try)

72 day scholars needed help, but only 23 hostel students. Day scholars need about three times as much help.

If you call the same share of each group (challenge 1), more of the day scholars who need help are left out. If you call enough day scholars to miss the same share (challenge 2), you must call many more day scholars than hostel students.

When two groups start from different situations, you cannot make every measure equal at the same time. Someone has to decide which one matters most. That decision is a human choice, not something the computer can make for you.

This is a different system. Here a high score is good for the student: it means the student is more likely to be shortlisted.

Why did this happen? (open after step 2)

The model copied the past shortlists. In the past, rural students were chosen less often, so the model learned “rural student: give a lower score”.

When you removed School type, the model still knew who was rural. In this data, city students travel about 20 minutes and rural students about 80 minutes. So travel time gave the same information. Information that secretly stands in for a hidden column is called a proxy.

Deleting a column does not guarantee a fair result. And deleting every related column is not automatically right either:

  • A long daily journey may really affect a student's studies. Should the model ignore it?
  • Marks and attendance may themselves reflect unequal chances, such as the quality of a student's school.

The real problem was in the past shortlists the model learned from. Amazon found the same thing in 2015 with a hiring tool that learned from ten years of mostly male resumes.
